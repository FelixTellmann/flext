First contact: what a proposal is, the new rule, and what to do with the old proposals

A plain-English walk through one decision. Every chart shows the same machinery from a different angle, so you can point at the thing you are choosing between.

What a proposal is

Every 15 minutes the engine looks at the four mailboxes. For each email that has not been looked at before, it runs the rules and decides what it would do with that email. It does not do it. It writes the decision down as a proposal: "if I were live, I would have moved this email to Quarantine".

Proposals pile up in a table. The Shadow Report page lists them, and you can approve any of them by hand, which moves the email. Nothing moves until either you approve it or the rule that made it has been switched on.

Switching a rule on (the engine calls this "auto") changes one thing: the engine picks up that rule's waiting proposals itself on each tick and carries them out, instead of waiting for you. That includes proposals the rule made before it was switched on; the backlog section below explains how far back that reaches.

The old rule and the new rule

First contact means the first email ever received from a sender you have never written to, where no rule names that sender.

The old rule, running on the server today: every first-contact email gets the proposal "move to Quarantine and mark it read". Nobody has switched that rule on, so no email has actually moved. The proposals have simply accumulated, and they include real people writing to you for the first time.

The new rule, being built now: a first-contact email is sorted into one of two shapes before anything is proposed.

  • Human-shaped: not a bulk or mailing-list email, not from an automated or no-reply sender, the signature on the email matches the sender's own domain, and it is addressed to you directly (in To, not only Cc or Bcc). These stay in the inbox. The proposal says "keep in inbox, reason: first contact from a person".
  • Machine-shaped: everything else. The proposal says "move to Quarantine, mark read", as before.

Then, for felix@tellmann.co.za only, the Quarantine half is switched on. Machine-shaped first contacts on that mailbox are moved to Quarantine within 15 minutes of arriving. Nothing is ever deleted; Quarantine is a folder you can open. The other three mailboxes stay proposal-only.

What happens to the old proposals (three options)

Here is the open question. The server already holds proposals made under the old rule. Every one of them says "Quarantine", including the ones for real people, because the old rule could not tell the difference. When the tellmann switch goes on, something has to be decided about those old proposals. There are three options. All three are shown against the same four parts: the old proposals, the 15-minute tick, the new tellmann switch, and the Quarantine folder.

One fact that shapes all three: the old proposals and the new machine-shaped proposals both say "Quarantine", and both come from the first-contact rule. The only thing that separates them is when they were decided. Option A depends on the new switch looking at that date; the other two options remove the old proposals so the date no longer matters.

Option A, recommended

Leave the old proposals alone; the switch only carries out proposals decided after the new rule goes live

The old proposals stay exactly where they are, as suggestions on the Shadow Report page. You can approve any of them by hand, or leave them. Nothing moves that you did not approve. The switch is built to ignore anything decided before the new code was deployed.

Downside: the Shadow Report page keeps the old rows around until you deal with them, and some of those rows are wrong under the new rule (they say Quarantine for people).

Option B

Delete the old first-contact proposals; the next tick re-decides those emails under the new rule

A script deletes the old first-contact proposals from the table. You are asked before it runs. Because the engine re-examines any email that has no proposal at all, the next 15-minute tick looks at each of those emails again and decides it under the new rule: human-shaped ones get "keep in inbox", machine-shaped ones get "Quarantine". On tellmann, where the switch is on, the machine-shaped ones are then moved.

Downside: a live delete on the production database; and on tellmann every machine-shaped old email is moved in bulk over the following ticks, not one at a time as they arrive. One more catch: the second look only happens for an email whose only proposal was the old first-contact one. An email that has since picked up another proposal (from the inbox sweeps, or from sitting in Junk) keeps that one and is not re-examined.

Option C

Rewrite the old proposals in place using the new rule

A script runs the new rule over each old proposal and changes what the proposal says. Nothing is deleted. Human-shaped ones now say "keep in inbox"; machine-shaped ones still say "Quarantine". On tellmann those are then moved by the switch.

Downside: the journal is history, and this rewrites history: a proposal that said Quarantine last week now says something else with no record of the change. Same bulk-move consequence on tellmann as option B.

Why A is the recommendation

A is the only option where nothing moves that you did not personally approve, and the only one that does not touch the database by hand. The cost is tidiness: old rows sit on the Shadow Report page, and some of them are wrong under the new rule. That is a cleanup problem, not a mail problem, and the simpler admin page in the last section is built to make that cleanup a few clicks. B and C both end with a bulk move on tellmann on the first ticks after the switch goes on, and once mail is in Quarantine you have to go looking for it.

Does switching a rule on also process its backlog?

Yes, for sender rules. Switching one on eventually carries out its whole backlog, oldest first, in batches.

Every one of the sender rules (around 113 of them) has been proposal-only for weeks, so the table holds thousands of proposals. It is natural to assume that switching a rule on only affects new mail. That is not what the code does. When a rule is on, each tick asks the table for that rule's proposals that have not been acted on yet, in that mailbox, sorted oldest first, and takes up to 200 of them. There is no date filter. A proposal from three weeks ago is just as eligible as one from three minutes ago; it is simply earlier in the queue.

Two details govern how fast that goes:

  • 200 per mailbox per tick, and that budget is shared across every rule that is on in that mailbox. If three rules are on, they share the 200 between them, in the order the rules happen to be listed.
  • Oldest first. A rule with 2,000 waiting proposals, alone at auto in its mailbox, clears in about 10 ticks, roughly two and a half hours. At 96 ticks a day one mailbox can work through up to about 19,000 proposals a day.

The first-contact rule is different, and that is why option A is even possible. The switch described above only works on sender rules, the ones that name an address or a domain. A first-contact proposal has no sender rule behind it (by definition, no rule names that sender), so today's switch cannot see first-contact proposals at all. The tellmann-only switch in this phase has to be built as its own small piece of machinery. Because it is new, it can be built to look at the decision date and ignore the old rows, which is what option A asks for. If it were built to behave exactly like the sender-rule switch, it would drain the old first-contact backlog too, oldest first, and that would move the old real-people emails into Quarantine on tellmann.

So the practical reading is: whenever you switch a sender rule on from the admin, expect its whole history of proposals to be carried out over the following hours, not just what arrives next. If that is not what you want for a given rule, the old proposals need dealing with first, using one of the same three shapes as above (leave and filter by date, delete and re-decide, or rewrite).

A simpler admin UI (proposal) proposal, not built

Today the admin is eight separate pages, and getting one rule from "proposal" to "on" means visiting three of them. The sketch below folds the routine work into one page called Inbox review. It is a wireframe: boxes and labels only, no styling decisions.

Pages it would fold together:

  • Needs Action
  • Senders (the rules list)
  • Mailboxes
  • Turn rules on (noisy, shows every rule with samples)
  • Stop mail arriving (read-only unsubscribe links)
  • Shadow Report (proposals to approve)
  • Action Journal
  • Filing Queue

The detail pages (Senders, Mailboxes, Journal, Filing Queue) would stay reachable for the rare deep dive. The point is that the Monday-morning pass never needs them.
